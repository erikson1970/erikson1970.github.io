    What this model is

    A minimal teaching model, not a design tool. Every number is a generic, publicly available, textbook-scale figure. The model is built to show why the 26 April 1986 excursion happened: the positive void coefficient at low power and low margin, the xenon pit after the power reduction, the flux pushed toward the bottom of the core, and control rods whose graphite displacers added reactivity in their first seconds of travel. It stops at the first steam explosion; nothing after that is modelled.

    How it works

    Neutronics. One point-kinetics amplitude with six delayed-neutron groups (β = 0.0048 for burned uranium–plutonium fuel, neutron generation time 1 ms), solved implicitly with time steps that shrink to a fraction of a millisecond when the core approaches prompt criticality. The core is split into four axial layers of 1.75 m. Each layer has its own steam void, fuel temperature, iodine and xenon, and rod composition. Their reactivities are weighted by the square of the local flux (importance), and the flux shape leans toward layers with more reactivity, within about half a second.

    Reactivity. Void: the whole-core worth of going from water to steam is about +4.5 β when only a handful of rods are in, falling to about +1.3 β with a normal rod margin, because absorbers compete with water for neutrons. Doppler: −1.2 × 10⁻⁵ per °C of fuel temperature. Xenon: about −2.8% Δk at full-power equilibrium, tracked per layer. Graphite temperature is held constant (its time constant is minutes). The power coefficient shown is computed, not entered: negative at full power, strongly positive at 200 MW with a low margin.

    Rods. 163 manual and emergency rods move as one bank; 12 regulating rods hold power in Auto. Each rod is a 6.2 m absorber above a 1.25 m gap and a 4.5 m graphite displacer. Fully withdrawn, the displacer sits in the middle of the core with 1.25 m of water below it. As a rod starts in, the displacer pushes that water out of the bottom of the channel (more reactivity) while the absorber only begins to enter the top. With the flux bottom-peaked, the first few seconds of a scram add reactivity. Full travel takes 18 s. The operating reactivity margin (ORM) is the importance-weighted absorber in the core, in equivalent fully inserted rods. The rules called for at least 15, and about 30 for normal operation.

    Circuit. One average boiling channel in eight axial nodes. Inlet water is drum water mixed with feedwater, so low feedwater and high flow put the inlet within a degree or two of boiling. Steam void comes from a drift-flux relation, which is steepest at qualities below 1%. Eight main circulation pumps in two loops share a common system curve; four of them are fed from turbine generator 8 and slow with it after its stop valves close. The drum has its own pressure and level, with dump and safety valves.

    The end point. The model declares a steam explosion when the hottest layer's fuel passes 1.0 MJ/kg or channel pressure passes 10 MPa. At that point pressure tubes rupture, the reactor space over-pressurises and the upper shield lifts.

    Two official views

    The first Soviet account and IAEA report INSAG-1 (1986) put most weight on operator violations: running below the test plan's power, too few rods in, safety signals bypassed. INSAG-7 (1992) revisited the evidence and found the design itself to be the root cause: the positive void coefficient, the positive scram effect, and an operating margin that the operators could not see live and that had not been flagged as a safety limit. Both reports criticised the safety culture of the time. This model tries to show both, without blaming individuals.

    Steam explosion

    Two plant workers died on the night of 26 April 1986. Of 134 people diagnosed with acute radiation syndrome, 28 died within months. Many more were affected, including thousands of thyroid cancers in people exposed as children, and the long-term toll is still studied. This model exists to understand how it happened and how RBMK reactors were changed afterwards.
