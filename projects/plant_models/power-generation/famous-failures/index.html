Famous failures

How plants failed, rebuilt so you can step through the sequence and try the counterfactuals.

Models

  • RBMK-1000 Night Shiftv1.0

    Chernobyl Unit 4, Ukrainian SSR · 26 April 1986

    A minimal model of the 26 April 1986 Chernobyl excursion: positive void coefficient, the xenon pit after the power reduction, the operating reactivity margin, and rods whose graphite tips add reactivity in their first seconds. Replay the night with captions and alternative histories, or run the plant yourself. Stops at the first steam explosion.

To do

  • SL-1TODO

    Idaho National Reactor Testing Station · 3 January 1961

    An Army Stationary Low-Power reactor. During maintenance, its single central control rod was withdrawn about 50 cm when only about 10 cm was needed. The reactor went prompt critical in milliseconds and the steam explosion killed the three-man crew. It is the only fatal reactor accident in the United States, and the reason a modern core must still shut down with its most reactive rod stuck out.

  • EBR-I partial meltdownTODO

    Idaho National Reactor Testing Station · 29 November 1955

    The first reactor to make usable electricity (1951) and to breed fuel. In a planned test with coolant flow off, the operator scrammed late with the slow rods instead of the fast ones. Inward bowing of the fuel rods added positive reactivity, and about half the sodium-potassium-cooled core melted.

  • BORAX-ITODO

    Idaho National Reactor Testing Station · 22 July 1954

    A boiling-water reactor destroyed on purpose: its control rod was ejected to see what a large power excursion would do. The resulting steam explosion threw core debris across the site. It proved that boiling-water reactors limit themselves through void feedback, and showed where that limit ends.

  • Three Mile Island Unit 2TODO

    Pennsylvania, USA · 28 March 1979

    A PWR loss-of-coolant accident through a pilot-operated relief valve stuck open. The control-room indicator showed the valve closed. A rising pressurizer level made the operators throttle back emergency injection while the core was partly uncovered, and about half the core melted. A good fit for the existing PWR models.

  • Windscale fireTODO

    Cumbria, UK · 10 October 1957

    An air-cooled graphite plutonium pile. A routine anneal to release stored Wigner energy in the graphite overheated fuel cartridges, which caught fire. The fire burned for about two days before the pile was flooded with water.

  • NRX accidentTODO

    Chalk River, Canada · 12 December 1952

    A heavy-water research reactor. Mistakes in operating the control rods during a low-power test led to a power excursion, a partial meltdown and a hydrogen explosion. A young US Navy officer, Jimmy Carter, helped with the clean-up.

  • Fermi 1 partial meltdownTODO

    Michigan, USA · 5 October 1966

    A sodium-cooled fast breeder. A zirconium liner plate added late as a "safety" feature broke loose and blocked coolant flow to part of the core, and two fuel subassemblies melted.

  • LucensTODO

    Vaud, Switzerland · 21 January 1969

    An experimental gas-cooled, heavy-water-moderated reactor built in a cavern. Corrosion from moisture blocked coolant channels in a fuel element, and the element overheated, melted and ruptured its pressure tube.

  • Fukushima DaiichiTODO

    Japan · 11 March 2011

    Earthquake, then tsunami: station blackout at a six-unit boiling-water reactor site. With no decay-heat removal, three cores melted and hydrogen explosions destroyed three reactor buildings. A decay-heat and station-blackout model.

  • Sayano–ShushenskayaTODO

    Khakassia, Russia · 17 August 2009

    A non-nuclear failure: at Russia's largest hydroelectric plant, fatigue cracks in the turbine-cover bolts let a turbine blow out of its pit under full water pressure. The flood killed 75 people and wrecked the turbine hall.

The to-do list is a starting point, not a ranking. Each model aims to let you step through the sequence of events and try the counterfactuals.

Each model is a single self-contained file that runs in the browser: no server, no install. All plant values are generic, textbook-scale figures chosen for teaching, derived from published, publicly available sources.
