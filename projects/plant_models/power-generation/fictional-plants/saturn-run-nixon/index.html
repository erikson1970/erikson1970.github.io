Fictional plants · Saturn Run (John Sandford and Ctein, 2015)

The Nixon's Power Plant v1.0

The American ship in Saturn Run drives VASIMR plasma engines from two huge nuclear reactors. It throws away about 9 GW of waste heat through a sail of molten-metal ribbons at 600 °C. The authors worked the numbers in spreadsheets. This page rebuilds the plant and the burn budget, then checks the one figure the physics pushes back on.

What the book says, and what the model does

From the authors' note.

  • Reactors and conversion: two reactor cores, each about four thousand times NASA's 2.5 MW "coffee-can" lithium-cooled reactor of the 1970s, so about 10 GW of heat apiece. A little more than half of the heat becomes electricity for the VASIMR engines. The rest, "nine gigawatts or so," must be radiated away.
  • Radiator: at 300 K the job would take about 9 km² of radiator. At 600 °C each square metre sheds about 65 kW. Molten-metal ribbons, extruded with a thin frozen skin, solidify in flight before collection beams catch them.
  • Water and exhaust: water is the reaction mass. The oxygen-to-hydrogen mix tunes the exhaust velocity.
  • The voyage: by spreadsheet, a mass ratio of about 10 with exhaust velocity varied from 35 to 300 km/s reaches Saturn in four to five months.

The plant. Reactor heat goes through a liquid-metal loop to a recuperated closed Brayton cycle (helium–xenon), the usual choice for space power. The pressure ratio is chosen for the best efficiency. Rejected heat goes to the radiator alloy, which freezes on the ribbons just below its melting point. Each square metre of ribbon radiates from both faces at 2εσT⁴. The required alloy flow is the waste heat divided by its latent heat. The freezing time is the ribbon mass per area times latent heat, divided by that flux. Ribbon speed and freezing time then set the sail's length and width. In Author's numbers mode the conversion efficiency is fixed at the book's 55%. In Brayton physics mode it comes from the temperatures.

The Carnot check. Rejecting heat at about 910 K (the 605 °C ribbons plus a heat-exchanger approach) caps any engine at 1 − 910 / Thot. Reaching 55% needs at least about 2,020 K (about 1,750 °C) at the hot end with a perfect engine. A realistic Brayton cycle needs far more, beyond any reactor material known. At the 1,300–1,500 °C a lithium-cooled refractory reactor might reach, the cycle gives only about 3–12%. The waste heat is then not 9 GW but about 17 GW, more than the sail can take. In the book's future the authors extrapolated a century of power-plant trends; this page shows how far that extrapolation reaches.

The voyage. A straight-line accelerate, flip, decelerate budget from Earth to Saturn. The Sun's gravity is ignored, so this is not the authors' Orbiter trajectory. Fixed Δv allowances cover spiralling out of Earth orbit and into Saturn orbit. The VASIMR holds a constant acceleration by trading exhaust velocity for thrust (thrust = 2ηP / ve) within its exhaust-velocity range. After the flip it brakes only as hard as needed to stop at Saturn. The page searches for the fastest acceleration that still arrives with the dry ship intact.
