How the model works

The cycle. In every machine here, a refrigerant boils in the evaporator and takes heat from the cold space. A second substance soaks up that vapour at low pressure: a salt solution (absorption) or a porous solid (adsorption). Heat in the generator drives the refrigerant back out at high pressure, and it condenses. The absorber and condenser reject heat to the sink. There is no compressor; heat does the work. Einstein and Szilard's 1930 design, and the Platen–Munters design in RV fridges, go further. A third gas keeps the total pressure the same everywhere, and a bubble pump lifts the solution, so nothing moves except fluid.

Temperature lift. A heat-driven machine needs its driving temperature lift (generator above sink) to exceed its pumping lift (sink above cold space) by a factor set by the working pair. The model uses the characteristic-temperature form fitted to manufacturers' data (Δ = Tgen − Tsink − B·(Tsink − Tcold)). Capacity rises with Δ above a small threshold. COP rises toward its asymptote and stays below the Carnot limit for a heat-driven chiller. Double-effect LiBr machines need about twice the lift: their high-pressure stage condenses refrigerant to fire the low-pressure stage.

Scaling. Each pair is anchored to a documented machine at its rated point. Temperature-driven chillers (hot water, steam) gain or lose capacity with Δ. Fired machines (gas, kerosene, electric element, RTG) have a fixed heat input per unit, so their capacity follows their COP. Machines are added in whole units to meet the load, and a source with limited heat (solar field, waste stream, RTG) caps the output.

Counterflow or parallel flow. Every exchange in the machine is between two streams: heating fluid and solution in the generator, cooling fluid and solution in the absorber, refrigerant vapour and the falling solution film, condensing and boiling refrigerant against water or air, and rich against poor solution in the solution heat exchanger. Each uses the ε-NTU relation for its arrangement. Counterflow is the rated design. Switching one exchanger to parallel flow lowers its effectiveness and widens its approach temperature, which eats into the lift margin Δ. Where one side boils or condenses at constant temperature (condenser, evaporator), the arrangement hardly matters; where both sides change temperature (generator, absorber, solution heat exchanger), it matters a lot. The diagram shows the direction of both streams inside every vessel.

Flows and faults. Each scenario fixes its number of machines at the design point, so a fault shows up as lost cooling. Its effects are behavioural, not a full simulation:

  • Low heating, cooling or chilled-water flow widens the stream's temperature change and lowers its film coefficient (h ∝ flow0.8).
  • Low chilled-water flow can freeze the refrigerant water and trip the machine.
  • Too little solution flow starves the absorber and, in LiBr machines, cools and concentrates the strong solution until it crystallizes, unless the anti-crystallization control holds the generator back.
  • Too much solution flow carries sensible heat around the loop and lowers COP.
  • Diffusion machines stall when the bubble pump gets too little heat or the fridge is out of level. Their pressure-gas charge sets the hottest sink they can work against.
  • Air leaking into vacuum machines blankets the absorber.
  • An under-cooled rectifier lets water into an ammonia evaporator.

Tubes and mixtures. The numbered tubes carry refrigerant vapour, condensate, rich solution and poor solution (plus the gas mixtures of the diffusion machines). Solution compositions for LiBr come from the Dühring equation. The ammonia–water figures and the gas-mixture fractions (from the partial pressures) are indicative.

LiBr–water details. Solution concentrations come from the ASHRAE Dühring equation for LiBr–water. The crystallization line is an approximate solubility curve (62.5% at 20 °C, about 72% at 100 °C). If the strong solution would cross it at the cold end of the solution heat exchanger, the model lowers the generator temperature, as the chiller's anti-crystallization control does. Water is the refrigerant, so LiBr chillers cannot go below about 5 °C.

Diffusion machines. In the Platen–Munters and Einstein–Szilard designs the total pressure is fixed by the charge. When the sink gets too hot, the condenser can no longer condense the refrigerant at that pressure and cooling collapses, which is why RV fridges struggle on very hot days. The model derates these machines over 5 °C below a maximum ambient.

Not modelled. Start-up, transients and time-dependent bed or solution dynamics (faults are evaluated as steady states), part-load cycling losses, frosting, and the detailed thermodynamic property models of ammonia–water and butane–ammonia–water (these pairs use fitted characteristics, not equations of state).

Where the numbers come from

    Other combinations worth knowing

    Working pairs: zeolite–water (InvenSor, drives from 45–100 °C), water–lithium chloride (three-phase storage machines), ammonia–lithium nitrate and ammonia–sodium thiocyanate (air-cooled, no rectifier), CaCl₂–ammonia chemisorption (deep freezing), and GAX and triple-effect LiBr cycles (COP up to about 1.7).

    Heat sources not modelled: biogas and landfill gas, fuel-cell exhaust, and nuclear process heat from small reactors.

    Heat sinks not modelled: hybrid wet/dry coolers and adiabatic pre-cooled air coolers. With the heating-loop sink selected, the machine is an absorption heat pump: its useful heat output is about 1 + COP times the heat input.
