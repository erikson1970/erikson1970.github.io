How thermoacoustics works

A parcel of gas is a tiny piston engine. In a sound wave, each parcel of gas moves back and forth and is squeezed and released at the same time. Next to a solid plate, it can swap heat with the plate while it moves. Whether it pumps heat or makes sound depends on the plate's temperature gradient compared with the critical gradient: the gradient that matches the parcel's own temperature swing from compression.

  • Below the critical gradient (Γ < 1): the parcel arrives hot from compression, dumps heat to the plate, moves back, expands cold and takes heat. Heat is pumped toward the pressure antinode. That is a refrigerator, and it absorbs acoustic power.
  • Above it (Γ > 1): the plate is hotter than the parcel. The parcel takes heat at high pressure and gives it up at low pressure, so it does net work on the wave. That is an engine, and it makes sound.

The plates must be spaced a few thermal penetration depths apart (δκ, a fraction of a millimetre in pressurized helium). Too close and the gas is locked to the plate's temperature; too far and most of it never feels the plate.

Standing waves and traveling waves. In a standing wave, pressure and velocity are a quarter-cycle apart. Heat transfer has to be imperfect for any heat to move, which makes these devices inherently irreversible (typically 5–20% of Carnot). In a traveling wave, pressure and velocity are in phase, and the gas in a fine-mesh regenerator follows a true Stirling cycle. That is why Backhaus and Swift's 1999 traveling-wave engine reached 30% efficiency. Its feedback loop lets gas stream steadily around the torus (Gedeon streaming), carrying heat away. A jet pump acts as a gas diode, with an asymmetric flow resistance that stops it.

The model. Standing-wave stacks use the short-stack, boundary-layer theory of Swift (1988), normalized as in Tijani et al. (2002), in a half-wave resonator. It includes the Prandtl-number corrections, conduction down the stack, and thermoviscous losses on the resonator walls. For Tijani's published refrigerator (helium, 10 bar, 400 Hz, 2% drive) it predicts the cooling running out at about −62 °C, against the −65 °C measured. The traveling-wave engine and the pulse-tube liquefier are lumped models calibrated to the published machines. Nonlinear effects (turbulence, harmonics, streaming in stacks) are represented only by a saturation term that sets the engine's steady amplitude.
