Epicycle Airframes

Three ways to turn a mesh into closed 3D curves whose x, y and z are each a Fourier series. Every term is a tilted ellipse turning at k revolutions per period, so a truncated series is a chain of 3D epicycles. The question is how few coefficients reach a given fraction of the airframe, and how that compares with storing the shape as a mesh or as a CAD script.

Coverage per coefficient

Bytes against other descriptions

Same yardstick everywhere: a surface counts as described where it lies within ε of the representation. Meshes and the CAD script describe a surface, so they get two numbers, recall (real skin near the model) and precision (model near the real skin). Wires get coverage and on-skin fraction. Wire coefficients are quantized and gzipped; quantization noise adds up across harmonics, so long single curves need a finer step than short loops. The slice-loop wire is listed at four coefficient encodings, from raw float32 down to gzipped fixed-point.

What came out

1 · surface walk

Cheap to trace, expensive to encode

An unbiased walk needs 39–64 bbox-diagonals of wire to reach 90%. Steering toward uncovered skin, with a jump to the nearest bare patch when stuck, cuts that to 19–24, close to the ideal lawnmower length of area ÷ 2ε. Either way the path is broadband, so 90% coverage costs 4,000–12,000 parameters, and it stays on the skin only because every short step is kept.

2 · plane-dominant spirals

A carrier with sidebands

Each sweep is one helical turn per slice, so its spectrum sits on a carrier at the turn count with sidebands from the cross-section shape, like AM. Following one blob per slice caps coverage at 75–87%: engines, stabilizers and wingtips fall off whichever blob is largest. The morph between dissimilar sections also lifts 5–9% of the wire off the skin.

3 · per-blob slice loops

The clear winner

Every cross-section blob becomes its own short chain, and most need only 1–3 harmonics, often a single tilted ellipse. 90% coverage takes 1,300–1,800 parameters, 95% about 1,500–2,100, with the whole wire on the skin. Splicing the same loops into one continuous curve costs roughly the same at 85–90% but tops out near 96%.

spacing

Pitch sets the spacing, harmonics set the fidelity

Slices and spiral turns sit 2ε apart because a wire paints a band 2ε wide. More coefficients make a curve follow its own path more closely; they do not move the slices together. To tighten, slice at a smaller pitch. For loops the cost grows with the loop count: 200 loops, drawing on planes halfway between, take 4,100–7,500 parameters and cover 95–99% of the skin at ε/2. For a spiral, halving the pitch doubles the turns, and the carrier frequency with them, so it needs about twice the harmonics.

crossing rule

Ties the rings together, at a price

Sweeping a slice plane along each axis finds 33–120 points where the section changes topology: blobs appear or vanish at extrema and merge or split at saddles. Forcing a 90° crossing loop through each takes 16–40 loops and reaches only 55–77% coverage, because crossings are long outlines that need many harmonics. 95% then costs 18–56% more parameters than plain slicing. What the rule buys is structure: the wire set at 95% falls from 31–44 separate rings to 1–7 connected pieces. Offered the same crossings without the rule, the greedy picker takes 11–38% of them and saves at most 1%.

crossings, one chain

Free once the loops are spliced

Joining the crossing-rule loops into a single series changes the sums. Crossings touch the rings they cross, so the out-and-back bridges shrink from 1.4–1.7 bbox-diagonals of doubled wire to 0.1–0.5. The curve reaches 90% with about 1,200–1,800 parameters, the same as the plain one-chain curve or fewer on every aircraft. The wire is 17–48% longer and coverage still tops out near 95%. Being one series, it samples at inverse-FFT speed.

time locality

Nearby times are nearby places

Points within 1% of the period of each other sit in a patch whose radius of gyration is 3–6% of the bbox diagonal, against about 20% for points taken from anywhere on the curve. That holds for every single-curve construction; 1% of the period is roughly one fuselage ring. The plain one-chain curve is the most local, 6–8% across a 5% window. Crossings stretch that to 10–15%, since each one runs the length of a wing or the fuselage. A range of times therefore works as a rough address for a region of the airframe.

locality, reversed

Nearby places are not nearby times

The converse fails. Take each point's neighbours within 2ε on the skin and find the one farthest away in time. On the plain one-chain curve 30–45% of points have a neighbour more than 5% of the period away. With crossings it is 63–88%, on the steered walk 68–87% and on the spirals 86–96%, where the typical gap is a third of the period. A curve is one-dimensional and the skin is two-dimensional, so no continuous covering curve can keep every neighbour close in time. A time range addresses a patch, but a patch maps to several separate time ranges.

sampling

Fast when the times are evenly spaced

Any time t gives a point on the wire, and the wire lies on the skin. Drawing 100,000 points in NumPy, the mesh gives 2.6–5.0 million points a second and the slice loops 5–8 million, since most loops are one to three harmonics. Every loop has its own harmonic count, set by how complicated its section is: 38–57% are a single ellipse and the longest need 18–33, so sampling a loop set is one small transform per loop. One long series at random times is slow, about 0.25 million a second at 300 harmonics and 0.03 million at 3,000, because every point sums every harmonic. At evenly spaced times one inverse FFT returns 25–49 million a second whatever the harmonic count. The points fall along the wire, not evenly over the area.

tilted planes

No consistent gain

Offering the greedy picker 24 plane orientations instead of three axes barely changed the parameter count at 90%: 2% lower on the B-17 and 7% lower on the King Air, but 2–24% higher on the other three. Airframes are built from axis-aligned parts, and greedy cost-per-gain favours many cheap ellipses. Tilt should matter for organic shapes, where a seahorse curl has no preferred axis.

float16

Half the bytes, same coverage

Storing each coefficient as float16 instead of float32 halves the slice-loop wire, from 6.1–8.4 KB to 3.1–4.2 KB, and coverage moves by at most 0.1 point. Gzip takes float16 down to 1.8–2.6 KB. Fixed-point with gzip is smaller again at 1.0–1.5 KB, because a half-float still carries precision that a 1% tolerance never uses.

sizes

Compressed meshes come out smaller

The best wire encoding, at about 95% coverage, is 1.0–1.5 KB. A decimated, Draco-encoded mesh describes the full surface to 99% in 265–451 B, and the fitted 981-byte OpenSCAD A319 (499 B gzipped) reaches 96% recall. The King Air script is 4.9 KB (1.6 KB gzipped) and exact, since its STL is that script's own render. Switch on the Draco overlay in the bench to see what 130–270 triangles look like. The raw STLs are 0.5–6 MB.

the caveat

A wire is not a surface

Coverage says every covered skin point is near some wire, but turning wires back into a skin needs a lofting step that isn't counted here. ε = 1% is also forgiving. At tighter tolerances all three families grow, and the decimated mesh's advantage is likely to widen because its error falls with the square of edge length.

Where this could be useful

Ideas that follow from the measurements. None of them has been built or tested here.

wire forming

One uncut toolpath

A single closed series is a path a wire bender or a freeform extruder can follow without lifting. Truncating the series caps the curvature, so the tightest bend can be set by choosing N. The spliced-loop curve walks every bridge twice, which a real wire would double up. The spiral has no bridges and behaves like vase-mode printing or filament winding.

frames

Ribs and stringers

Slice loops are the formers and ribs of an airframe; crossing loops are its stringers. Rings alone are loose parts. The crossing rule turns them into one frame with a joint wherever two loops meet, and the critical points say where the joints matter most.

antenna models

A wire grid in a kilobyte

Method-of-moments codes such as NEC model a conducting body as a grid of wires, running both ways so current can flow in either direction, spaced near a tenth of a wavelength. Slice loops plus crossings are that grid, with the pitch as the one setting to match to frequency.

level of detail

Any prefix is a model

Harmonics are ordered by frequency and loops by coverage gained, so the first part of the file is already a coarser aircraft. The sliders in the bench are that truncation. It suits streaming, previews and matching detail to distance. Time locality adds a second axis: a block of consecutive samples from a single chain is one patch of the aircraft, so regions can be drawn or sent separately. Going the other way, from a place to its samples, needs a small index of time ranges.

analytic geometry

Tangents and normals for free

Tangent, curvature and length come straight from the coefficients. On a slice loop the outward normal is the tangent crossed with the slice-plane normal, enough to drop points that face away from a viewer. Telling whether another part of the aircraft is in the way still needs a surface, which a wire does not have.

signatures and paths

Area curves and scan routes

The first harmonic of each fuselage slice is an ellipse, and its area along the body is the cross-section area distribution. Harmonic magnitudes do not change when a loop is re-timed, which makes them usable for matching shapes. The steered walk and the spirals are also coverage routes for a scanner or spray head, with ε as the footprint.

Method

  • Each mesh rotated to X = fuselage, Y = span, Z = up, centred, and scaled so the bounding-box diagonal is 1. ε = 0.01.
  • Coverage uses 30,000 area-uniform skin samples. The on-skin fraction is measured against a 200,000-point cloud.
  • Curves are resampled by arc length, then each coordinate goes through an FFT. Harmonic k contributes Ak cos kt + Bk sin kt with Ak, Bk ∈ ℝ³.
  • Slice loops use the smallest N whose reconstruction stays within ε/2 of the section. Greedy selection maximizes newly covered skin per parameter.
  • The spliced variant joins loops at their closest point pair with an out-and-back bridge, then refits one series.
  • Tighter spacing: once coverage at ε passes 97% the loop sets carry on to 200 loops, adding candidates from planes halfway between the first set and scoring them against ε/2.
  • Crossing rule: planes sweep X, Y and Z at the 2ε pitch and solid cross-section regions are matched between neighbouring slices by overlap. A region with no partner marks an extremum; two regions sharing a partner mark a saddle. Each such point gets candidate loops from the two planes through it that contain the sweep axis. Crossings are taken, best coverage per parameter first, until every point has one, and the usual greedy selection continues from there. Pieces are counted by joining loops that pass within ε of each other.
  • Time locality: each single curve is resampled to 4,096 points by arc length. The figure is the radius of gyration of the points inside a window of 1% or 5% of the period, median over windows, compared with the radius of gyration of the whole curve. The reverse measure takes, for each of those points, the largest time gap to any other point within 2ε. Bridge wire is the spliced curve's length minus the summed length of its loops.
  • Sampling speed: 100,000 points per draw, NumPy on one core, best of three. The mesh draw is trimesh's area-weighted sampler. Loop draws pick a loop in proportion to its length. Timings are for this machine and only their ratios carry over.
  • Wire encodings: float32 and float16 store each coefficient directly, plus one byte per chain for its harmonic count. Fixed-point rounds every coefficient to a multiple of one step, the coarsest of ε, ε/2, ε/4 and so on that loses under half a point of coverage and of on-skin fraction, then gzips.
  • Mesh baselines: STL as supplied, GLB from trimesh, Draco at the lowest quantization that holds the target fidelity. The decimated rows use MeshLab's quadric edge collapse on both the supplied mesh and a 0.003-pitch voxel remesh, keep whichever reaches fewer faces, then Draco. The bench overlay draws the vertices and faces exactly as the Draco decoder returns them.
  • The OpenSCAD A319 is hand-built from about 40 measured parameters, then nudged by coordinate search against the same metric. Rendered with OpenSCAD itself it scores 95.7% recall and 93.3% precision. The B-17 script was written separately and never fitted to this mesh, so its score shows only what an unfitted script gets. The King Air mesh is the direct output of its script, a hollow shell with window cut-outs, so that script scores 100% on both.
